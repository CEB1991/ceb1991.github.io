A proposal, based on public information only

What I would do in the first 90 days, and what I would refuse to rush.

A new coordinator arrives under pressure to produce something visible fast. The trap is publishing guidance in month one that nobody helped write, then spending a year explaining why districts ignored it. This plan spends the first month listening on purpose, and it ends at day 90 with finished drafts in the approval path rather than a publication date I do not control.

Days 1 to 30

Listen inside first

The county office is already doing pieces of this work. There is a task force, there are divisions carrying their own AI questions, and there are teams already delivering professional learning. Before I ask forty two districts anything, I should be able to describe accurately what SDCOE already does, what it has already committed to, and who has already decided what.

  • Meet internally first, across divisions: Integrated Technology Services, cybersecurity, legal, purchasing, communications, human resources, and the teams already running AI professional learning. Ask each one what they have already promised, what they are already fielding, and what they would want from this role.
  • Meet the existing task force and working groups, read every prior artifact, and understand what has already been said to whom.
  • Read Board Policy 0441 and the board's policy record alongside the CDE guidance, released January 2026, and the CDE model policy, released June 2026, and mark the decisions each district still has to make locally.2
  • Then go outside. Interview a deliberately uneven sample: a large unified district, two small districts, a charter network, a juvenile court and community schools site, and at least two people whose job is not technology.
  • Run a short internal survey, in English and in Spanish, on how county office staff already use these tools. Not whether they should. How they already do.
  • Build an honest inventory of what is in use, including the tools nobody approved and nobody procured. Shadow use is not a discipline problem, it is a signal about what people needed and did not get.
  • Run a short countywide readiness survey. Ask what tools are already in use, what is already prohibited, what people are afraid of, and what they want help with first. Publish the results back to the people who answered.

What exists at day 30: a written landscape summary, a stakeholder map, an inventory and risk register of the tools already in use, survey results with response rates by district size, and a short list of the three problems people named most often.

Why the survey goes out first

It does two jobs. It gives me a baseline I can measure against in a year, and it tells several hundred people that the new coordinator asked before telling. That second job is worth more.

Days 31 to 60

Translate policy into practice

The county board has taken up Board Policy 0441, Artificial Intelligence, on the public agenda, on a board that meets once a month and reads policy items more than once.1 That shapes this phase. A policy of this kind sets principles for how AI is used if it is used, rather than endorsing or requiring any tool, which is also how the state frames its own model policy: exemplary, not mandatory.2 So the open question is not what we believe. It is what a site administrator actually does on a Tuesday, and what support a district gets from the county office to do it.

  • Bring the task force a proposed charter: what the group decides, what it advises on, who it reports to, how often it meets, and how it will make decisions when members disagree. Governance bodies fail on ambiguity more than on disagreement.
  • Draft the first two artifacts districts asked for. A board policy states principles; it does not tell anyone whether a specific tool may touch student data. That gap is likely filled by a plain language practice guide keyed to both BP 0441 and the CDE model policy, naming three approved use cases and three clear red lines, and a tool review checklist covering SOPIPA and AB 1584 contract terms, data retention, and training use.
  • Circulate drafts as drafts, with a comment deadline and named credit for contributors, and test them against the staff who have to live by them rather than only the people who wrote them. Guidance written in public gets adopted. Guidance revealed finished gets forwarded and forgotten.
  • Design the professional learning sequence rather than a one off workshop. A first session for staff, a leadership session for cabinets and boards, and a family session in English and Spanish.
  • Agree on the measures now, before there is anything to defend: reach, adoption, satisfaction, and at least one measure of harm avoided or risk reduced. Agree at the same time how this gets reported back to a board that will want to know what districts and students are actually experiencing, not just what was published.

What exists at day 60: a task force charter, two circulating drafts with named contributors, a professional learning calendar, and an agreed measurement plan.

Policy is not practice

A board can adopt a policy in one evening. Translating it into something a 400 student district can act on without a technology director is a year of work, and it is the part that decides whether the policy means anything.

Days 61 to 90

Pilot, then recommend

This is where a 90 day plan usually overpromises. Anything that carries the county office name goes through a review path I do not control the calendar for, and anything that belongs in board policy moves at the speed of a board that meets once a month and reads most items twice. So the honest commitment at day 90 is finished work submitted, not published work. What I can commit to is that nothing sits waiting on me.

  • Finish both artifacts to review ready: dated, version numbered, with a stated review cycle and a named owner, and submit them through whatever internal path applies, communications, legal, and cabinet. Guidance without a version number and a review date is guidance nobody will trust in eighteen months.
  • Put the approval path in writing alongside them: who reviews, in what order, what would need to go to the board and what would not, and the realistic date each one becomes public. A schedule everyone can see is worth more than a surprise launch.
  • Deliver the first professional learning sessions, which do not require anyone's approval to be useful, including one family session in Spanish designed for that audience rather than translated into it.
  • Run two small supervised pilots with named owners, a defined end date, and real measurement, chosen because they are boring and useful. Operational, human reviewed, no student data in them, so the first thing the county says publicly about its own AI use is modest and verifiable.
  • Bring leadership a recommendation package rather than a status update: what the pilots showed, including what did not work, and what I would do about vendor review and ongoing governance.
  • Draft the public information page, what is approved, what is not, who to ask, how to report a problem, and take it as far as the review path allows within the window.
  • Report back to the task force and to the people who took the survey, including what did not work and what is still waiting on a decision.

What exists at day 90: five things. A plain language practice guide tied to Board Policy 0441, review ready and submitted with a written approval timeline. An inventory and risk register of the tools already in use. Results from two supervised pilots, including what did not work. A recommendation on vendor review and ongoing governance. And a professional learning plan for the year ahead, built from what the sessions and the survey actually showed.

Why day 90 is not a publication date

The county board holds its regular business meetings once a month, on the second Wednesday.1 A policy item is normally read more than once before it is adopted. That is one policy, two meetings, on a monthly calendar, and any guidance that depends on an adopted policy inherits that clock. I would rather show a panel a plan that respects the approval calendar than one that quietly assumes it away.

What I would deliberately not do in 90 days

  • Not endorse a vendor. An early countywide endorsement locks in a market position before anyone has evidence, and it is very hard to reverse.
  • Not put AI anywhere near a consequential student decision. Not identification, not discipline, not eligibility, not grades. Not in year one.
  • Not promise a countywide policy. Districts adopt their own policies. A county office that writes theirs for them creates a document with no local ownership and no legal standing.
  • Not measure success by adoption rate. If the honest finding is that a use case is not worth it, the system needs to be able to hear that from me.

Sources

  1. San Diego County Board of Education, board page and agendas and minutes. Regular business meetings are held monthly, on the second Wednesday; agenda packets and policy readings are posted there.
  2. California Department of Education, release of California's AI in education guidance, January 2026, and the model policy on artificial intelligence in education, issued under SB 1288 (Becker, 2024) and published as exemplary rather than mandatory.

This plan is written from publicly available information about county office structure and California requirements. It is a proposal, not a commitment on behalf of any agency, and the first thing I would expect is for the listening phase to change it.

Practice, and a session you could run tomorrow

One session cannot serve a first timer and a daily user. So I would run two.

The rooms I teach now are genuinely mixed, a program secretary and a systems analyst learning the same reporting system in the same hour, and mixing works there because everyone needs the same submission deadline met. AI is different. The gap between someone opening a chat window for the first time and someone already automating their week is too wide to bridge in sixty minutes. So the design I would bring is two workshops, a beginner one and an intermediate one, each planned in full, with people choosing which one they belong in.

Five things I will do in every session

Start with the work, not the technology

Plenty of people arrive genuinely curious about AI, and a good number arrive already using it. What they usually do not arrive with is a specific place to put it in their own week. So I open with the task, the one that eats a Thursday and should take twenty minutes, and introduce the tool as an answer to it. Curiosity brings people in the door. A task they recognize is what keeps the content from evaporating by Monday.

Name the failure modes before the features

If people hear what a tool gets wrong first, they trust the demo more, not less, and they use it more carefully afterward. It also inoculates the room against the vendor demo they will see next month.

Everybody touches it in the first fifteen minutes

Watching a facilitator drive teaches almost nothing. I would rather cover half the content and have every person do the thing once, badly, with me in the room to see where it goes wrong.

Two levels, chosen by the participant, never assigned

A beginner workshop and an intermediate workshop, advertised with plain descriptions of what each one assumes, so people place themselves. Nobody is told in front of colleagues which group they belong in, a supervisor does not decide it for them, and anyone who guesses wrong can walk into the other one next month. Sorting by level is what makes depth possible for the people who want it. Letting people sort themselves is what keeps it from being a judgment.

Leave with one artifact and one boundary

One thing they built that they will actually reuse, and one clear rule about what not to put in the tool. If a participant remembers only two things from an hour, those are the right two.

Where this comes from

I design and deliver professional learning on state and federal reporting systems to teachers, site administrators, family childcare business owners, clerical staff, systems technicians, and analysts, in the same room. These are the moves that survive contact with that audience. My AI teaching so far has been smaller and closer in: my own department, in small groups, on tools we use in our own work. The plans below are what I would build to take it countywide.

Workshop one, beginner: AI without the hype, 60 minutes

For staff who have not used these tools, or have tried once and stopped. No prior experience assumed, any role. Room needs: devices, an approved tool, and a facilitator willing to show something failing in front of everyone.

Beginner workshop, 60 minutes, run as written.
TimeSegmentWhat happens, and why
0 to 5The task on the tableParticipants name one recurring task they would hand off today. Collected live on screen. This becomes the working material for the rest of the hour, so the session is about their work from minute one.
5 to 12What it is, in one plain explanationA large language model predicts likely next text from patterns in enormous amounts of writing. It does not look anything up unless it is connected to a source, and it does not know whether it is right. No jargon, no architecture diagrams, no anthropomorphizing.
12 to 22Watch it fail, on purposeFacilitator asks for something the tool will get confidently wrong: a specific local fact, a made up citation, a calculation. The room sees fluent and wrong in the same output. This is the anchor for everything after.
22 to 38Everyone tries itParticipants use their own task from segment one. Two paces, self selected: guided prompts to follow step by step, or an open attempt with a facilitator circulating. Success criteria are on the wall: a usable draft, and you can say what you would check before sending it.
38 to 48The rules, tied to what just happenedWhat may and may not go into the tool, connected to the actual mistakes that just occurred in the room. Student names, records, health and special education content, staff personnel matters, anything not already public. The rule lands because they just saw why.
48 to 56What it cost, and who is accountableBrief and honest: the license, the training time, the environmental footprint, and the standard that a named person is answerable for anything consequential. Treating this as ordinary content rather than a disclaimer is the point.
56 to 60One artifact, one boundary, one questionEach participant writes down the thing they will reuse, the rule they will follow, and one question they still have. The questions go into the next session and into the countywide guidance backlog.

Workshop two, intermediate: making it hold up, 60 minutes

For staff already using these tools weekly who want the output to be reliable rather than impressive. Prerequisite is use, not expertise. Participants bring one real recurring task and one attempt that disappointed them.

Intermediate workshop, 60 minutes, run as written.
TimeSegmentWhat happens, and why
0 to 6Bring your failureEach participant shows an attempt that did not work. Collected and grouped live. The room finds out quickly that the same four problems are everywhere, which does more for confidence than any reassurance from the front.
6 to 18Why that failed, specificallyThe four usual causes: missing context, no stated constraints, no example of what good looks like, and asking for a judgment the tool has no basis to make. Each one repaired live on a participant's own example rather than a clean demo.
18 to 34Build one reusable thingParticipants turn a recurring task into something durable: a saved prompt, a template, a checklist. It has to be specific enough that a colleague could run it. This is the segment that separates the second workshop from the first.
34 to 45Documents and data, safelyWhat may be uploaded, what has to be stripped first, and where the tool is most likely to be wrong in ways that read as right. Worked on real formats people actually handle: a spreadsheet, a letter, a report extract with the identifiers removed.
45 to 55Verification you would defendWhat checking actually means when you are the named human: what you verify, what you never take on trust, what you keep a record of, and what you say when someone asks whether AI wrote it. Ending on refusal cases, the tasks where the answer is do not use it.
55 to 60A three week commitmentEach participant names one workflow they will run for three weeks and the one thing they will measure: time, error rate, or whether they kept using it at all. Those become the agenda for the clinic.

Follow up that makes it stick

A single session changes nothing on its own. Three weeks later, a 20 minute optional clinic where people bring what broke. Attendance at the clinic is a better adoption measure than the satisfaction survey at the end of the session, and the questions raised there are the real curriculum for the next round, including which beginners are ready to move up.

District AI readiness self check

Ten questions a district team can answer in a 45 minute meeting. Nothing is stored or sent anywhere, and this runs entirely in your browser. Any answer other than yes is a starting point, not a failing grade. I would use this to decide who needs which kind of support, never to rank districts against each other.

Answer as a team, then read the summary

  • 1. Can we name every AI tool currently in use by staff, including free ones nobody procured?

    Shadow use is the risk that is already happening.

  • 2. Do we have a written, board adopted position, or are we operating on informal practice?

    Informal practice cannot be defended to a parent or an auditor.

  • 3. For each approved tool, do we know where data is stored, whether our data trains the model, and how long it is retained?

    If nobody can answer this, the tool is not approved, it is tolerated.

  • 4. Do our contracts include the student data terms California requires of vendors, and has someone actually read them?

    SOPIPA and AB 1584 put obligations in the contract, not the brochure.

  • 5. Does staff know, in one sentence they could repeat, what they may not put into these tools?

    A rule nobody can recite is not a control.

  • 6. Is there any place where an AI output currently influences a decision about a student, and if so, who is the named accountable human?

    This is the question with the most direct consequence for a child.

  • 7. What have we told families, in what languages, and did we invite questions or only inform?

    Informing is not engagement, and translation is not the same as design.

  • 8. Do we know what our students already do with these tools, rather than assuming?

    Policy written from assumption ages badly and fast.

  • 9. Is it clear who is responsible when something goes wrong, and does staff know how to reach them?

    Incidents get reported when reporting is easy and safe.

  • 10. Have we written down what would make us stop using a tool, before we need it?

    Stop conditions written under pressure are written badly.

0 of 10 answered yes. Ten questions still to answer.

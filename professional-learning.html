Practice, and a session you could run tomorrow

The hardest room is the mixed room, and it is the only room that matters.

In my current role a single session might hold a program secretary, a clerk, a systems analyst, and a director. Design for the analyst and you lose most of the room. Design for the newest person and the analyst checks out. Here is how I handle that, and a full session plan built the same way.

Five things I do in every session

Start with the work, not the technology

Nobody arrives wanting to learn about AI. They arrive wanting to stop spending Thursday on a task that should take twenty minutes. Open with the task, introduce the tool as an answer to it, and the technical content lands because there is a hook to hang it on.

Name the failure modes before the features

If people hear what a tool gets wrong first, they trust the demo more, not less, and they use it more carefully afterward. It also inoculates the room against the vendor demo they will see next month.

Everybody touches it in the first fifteen minutes

Watching a facilitator drive teaches almost nothing. I would rather cover half the content and have every person do the thing once, badly, with me in the room to see where it goes wrong.

Differentiate by giving people the choice, not by sorting them

Two tracks in the same room, chosen by the participant. Nobody gets told they are in the beginner group in front of their colleagues, and the people who want depth get depth.

Leave with one artifact and one boundary

One thing they built that they will actually reuse, and one clear rule about what not to put in the tool. If a participant remembers only two things from an hour, those are the right two.

Where this comes from

I design and deliver professional learning to more than 500 staff a year on state and federal reporting systems, across every classification from clerical to analyst. These are the moves that survive contact with that audience.

Sample session: AI without the hype, 60 minutes

Audience: school and district staff, no prior AI experience assumed, mixed roles. Room needs: devices, an approved tool, and a facilitator willing to show something failing.

Session plan, 60 minutes, designed for a mixed room.
TimeSegmentWhat happens, and why
0 to 5The task on the tableParticipants name one recurring task they would hand off today. Collected live on screen. This becomes the working material for the rest of the hour, so the session is about their work from minute one.
5 to 12What it is, in one plain explanationA large language model predicts likely next text from patterns in enormous amounts of writing. It does not look anything up unless it is connected to a source, and it does not know whether it is right. No jargon, no architecture diagrams, no anthropomorphizing.
12 to 22Watch it fail, on purposeFacilitator asks for something the tool will get confidently wrong: a specific local fact, a made up citation, a calculation. The room sees fluent and wrong in the same output. This is the anchor for everything after.
22 to 38Everyone tries itParticipants use their own task from segment one. Two tracks, self selected: guided prompts to follow, or an open build with a facilitator circulating. Success criteria are on the wall: usable draft, and you can say what you would check before sending it.
38 to 48The rules, tied to what just happenedWhat may and may not go into the tool, connected to the actual mistakes that just occurred in the room. Student names, records, health and special education content, staff personnel matters, anything not already public. The rule lands because they just saw why.
48 to 56What it cost, and who is accountableBrief and honest: the license, the training time, the environmental footprint, and the standard that a named person is answerable for anything consequential. Treating this as ordinary content rather than a disclaimer is the point.
56 to 60One artifact, one boundary, one questionEach participant writes down the thing they will reuse, the rule they will follow, and one question they still have. The questions go into the next session and into the countywide guidance backlog.

Follow up that makes it stick

A single session changes nothing on its own. Three weeks later, a 20 minute optional clinic where people bring what broke. Attendance at the clinic is a better adoption measure than the satisfaction survey at the end of the session, and the questions raised there are the real curriculum for round two.

District AI readiness self check

Ten questions a district team can answer in a 45 minute meeting. Nothing is stored or sent anywhere, and this runs entirely in your browser. Any answer other than yes is a starting point, not a failing grade. I would use this to decide who needs which kind of support, never to rank districts against each other.

Answer as a team, then read the summary

  • 1. Can we name every AI tool currently in use by staff, including free ones nobody procured?

    Shadow use is the risk that is already happening.

  • 2. Do we have a written, board adopted position, or are we operating on informal practice?

    Informal practice cannot be defended to a parent or an auditor.

  • 3. For each approved tool, do we know where data is stored, whether our data trains the model, and how long it is retained?

    If nobody can answer this, the tool is not approved, it is tolerated.

  • 4. Do our contracts include the student data terms California requires of vendors, and has someone actually read them?

    SOPIPA and AB 1584 put obligations in the contract, not the brochure.

  • 5. Does staff know, in one sentence they could repeat, what they may not put into these tools?

    A rule nobody can recite is not a control.

  • 6. Is there any place where an AI output currently influences a decision about a student, and if so, who is the named accountable human?

    This is the question with the most direct consequence for a child.

  • 7. What have we told families, in what languages, and did we invite questions or only inform?

    Informing is not engagement, and translation is not the same as design.

  • 8. Do we know what our students already do with these tools, rather than assuming?

    Policy written from assumption ages badly and fast.

  • 9. Is it clear who is responsible when something goes wrong, and does staff know how to reach them?

    Incidents get reported when reporting is easy and safe.

  • 10. Have we written down what would make us stop using a tool, before we need it?

    Stop conditions written under pressure are written badly.

0 of 10 answered yes. Ten questions still to answer.

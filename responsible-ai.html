A position, written by me

Responsible adoption means naming the costs out loud.

Most AI guidance for schools is a list of permissions. This is a list of obligations. Five commitments I would hold myself and any tool I recommend to, followed by a direct answer to the two objections that deserve the most respect: what this technology costs the environment, and what it costs students when it is wrong.

Five commitments

1. Purpose before tool

The question is never "should we use AI." It is "what problem are we solving, for whom, and what would success look like if we solved it a boring way instead." If a spreadsheet, a policy change, or hiring one person solves it better, that is the answer, and saying so publicly is what earns the credibility to say yes later.

In practice

Half the automation requests I get in my current role turn out to be process problems. Fixing the process is faster than automating the mess.

2. Privacy by default, not by exception

Student data privacy in California is not one rule, it is a stack: FERPA, COPPA and CIPA at the federal level, and SOPIPA and AB 1584 governing what vendors may do with student information and what must be in the contract. A tool that cannot answer where the data goes, who trains on it, how long it is retained, and how it is deleted does not get piloted, no matter how good the demo is. Free tools are the highest risk category here, because someone is paying, and if it is not the district then the payment is data.

3. A named human is accountable for every consequential output

Consequential means anything that touches a student's placement, discipline, eligibility, grade, or record, or anything that goes to a family with the county office name on it. For those, the standard is not "a human looked at it." It is that a specific named person is answerable for it and had the information and the authority to say no. Human in the loop stops being meaningful the moment the human's only real option is to click approve.

4. Honest accounting of what it costs

Money, staff attention, and environmental footprint, stated in the same document as the benefits. A pilot that saves 40 staff hours and quietly adds a recurring license, three hours of training per site, and an unmeasured energy cost has not been evaluated. It has been advertised.

5. Access without coercion

Equitable access means the districts with the least capacity get the most support, not the same PDF as everyone else. It also means no student or family is forced through an AI system to reach a human service, no staff member is evaluated on AI use, and there is always a path that does not involve the tool. Access and consent are the same commitment viewed from two directions.

The environmental question, answered honestly

This objection is often dismissed in education circles, and it should not be. Here is what the credible numbers actually say, including where they disagree.

At the global scale, the trend is real. The International Energy Agency projects that electricity consumption by data centres will roughly double from about 415 terawatt hours in 2024 to around 945 terawatt hours by 2030, slightly more than Japan's total electricity use today, with AI named as the most important driver.1 The IEA also puts that in proportion: data centres would be near 3 percent of global electricity demand in 2030, and the projected increase is smaller than the growth expected from electric vehicles or air conditioning over the same period.2 Both halves of that are true at once, and quoting only one half is how this argument usually goes wrong.

At the single prompt scale, the numbers are small and contested. Google published a methodology in 2025 estimating that a median text prompt to its assistant used about 0.24 watt hours of electricity, 0.03 grams of carbon dioxide equivalent, and 0.26 millilitres of water, roughly five drops.3 That is a vendor's own accounting of its own product, it covers inference and not model training, it is a median that hides a long tail, and outside analysts have pushed back on what it leaves out. Reported figures for other systems run several times higher, and longer outputs, reasoning modes, and image generation all cost materially more.4 The honest summary is that a single text prompt is a small unit of energy, that nobody outside the vendors can fully audit these numbers, and that scale is where the impact lives.

What I will not say

I will not tell a board member that AI is environmentally free, and I will not repeat the viral bottle-of-water figures that trace back to a single early estimate. Both moves cost credibility with people who read the underlying sources.

What a county office can actually control

We do not set data centre policy. We do control demand, procurement, and honesty, and those are not nothing.

  • Right size the model. Most school and district use cases are summarizing, drafting, translating, and formatting. Those do not need the largest and most expensive reasoning model. Choosing a smaller model for routine work is the single largest lever available to us, and it usually saves money at the same time.
  • Refuse always on features. AI features that run on every document, every message, or every student interaction by default generate enormous volumes of low value inference. Default off, on for a purpose.
  • Ask vendors the question in writing. Procurement can require disclosure of energy and water reporting, data centre regions, and whether our usage trains their models. Vendors answer questions that appear in contracts. If enough public agencies ask, disclosure improves.
  • Measure our own usage. An annual count of prompts, seats, and licenses in an AI report is more useful than a paragraph of reassurance, and it lets us say plainly when usage is growing faster than value.
  • Teach it. California defines AI literacy to include understanding AI's implications and ethical considerations. Environmental cost belongs in that curriculum, not in a separate protest. Students who ask what a prompt costs are doing exactly what we want them to do.

What this number does not include, and why I still show it

It excludes training the models, the electricity your own devices and network use, the embodied carbon of the hardware, and everything other than text. A reasoning request, a long document, or an image costs materially more than the median. The underlying figures are a vendor measuring its own product, they are a median that hides a long tail, and they cannot be independently audited.

So this is an order of magnitude, not an invoice. I show it anyway for one reason: a number on a slide starts a real conversation, and the conversation it starts is the right one. A few hundred kilowatt hours is not nothing and it is also not a catastrophe, which means the actual question is not the energy. It is whether the work being done with it was worth doing, and whether the same result could have come from a smaller model or no model at all.

The honest tension

There is no version of this where a county office adopts AI and has zero environmental footprint. There is a version where we adopt it deliberately, for uses that are worth it, at the smallest scale that works, with the cost written down where the public can see it. That is what I would commit to, and it is a stronger position than either denial or abstention.

Where this sits in California policy

SB 1288 (Becker, 2024) required the state superintendent to convene a working group and directed the California Department of Education to publish guidance on the safe use of AI in education by January 1, 2026, and a model policy for local educational agencies by July 1, 2026.5 CDE posted that model policy on June 25, 2026, and it is explicitly exemplary rather than mandatory under Education Code section 33308.5, which means local governing boards still have to decide, adapt, and adopt.6 AB 2876 (Berman, 2024) put AI literacy into the frame for curriculum, defined to include limitations, implications, and ethical considerations.7

That combination is exactly why a county office role like this exists. The state has produced a model, and 42 districts and 129 charter schools now have to turn a model into a local policy they can defend to their own boards and families. Helping them do that, without writing it for them, is the job.

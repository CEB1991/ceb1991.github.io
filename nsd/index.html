Candidate brief · Job announcement #2026-13

Did the day run?

Every technology decision a district makes is eventually judged by that one question. Not by the architecture diagram. By whether a first grade teacher could take attendance at 8:00 and get through the lesson she planned.

I am Carlos Beleche, and I am applying to lead Information Technology Services at National School District. This page is what I would bring to the role, written before anyone gave me access to your systems.

Ten years in K-12 and state government IT M.S. Cybersecurity & Operations Leadership Certified ScrumMaster Bilingual English / Spanish

The day this department protects

Identity and access

Sign-on has to work before the coffee does.

Single sign-on, account provisioning for new hires and substitutes, device readiness. When this breaks, it breaks for everyone at once, and it breaks at the worst possible hour. This is the least visible system in the district and the first one I would want a clean picture of.

Student information system

Attendance is a legal record and a funding record.

Enrollment, attendance, and state reporting sit on integrations that have to run correctly whether or not anyone is watching them. I have spent nine years inside California student data systems, including CALPADS, SEIS, and the SIS platforms districts run on.

Network, capacity, and filtering

Thirty devices in one room, ten schools at once.

Coverage in the classrooms that were built before wireless existed, content filtering that satisfies CIPA without blocking the lesson, and enough capacity that instruction does not queue. Teachers do not file tickets about bandwidth. They just stop planning lessons that need it.

Operational systems

The systems nobody calls technology.

Nutrition services, transportation, facilities, payroll. They rarely make the technology plan and they always make the emergency. A department that only serves the classroom has left half the district unsupported.

Availability and service levels

Testing windows do not move.

State assessment is the one week a year where uptime is measured publicly and remembered for years. Service level commitments should be written down before that week, not defended after it. I manage technology operations against contract and service level requirements today.

Communications and safety

2:30 is a communications problem.

Phones, intercom, mass notification, and the family messaging that has to reach every household in the language they speak at home. In a district where many families are more comfortable in Spanish, bilingual communication is not a courtesy feature. It is the requirement.

The work nobody sees

Patching, backups, tickets, and the E-Rate calendar.

Restore tests that actually restore. Vendor contracts read before they auto-renew. Funding year deadlines met early. This is the part of the job that produces no applause and prevents every disaster. It is the part I would protect time for.

A plan, offered with the appropriate humility

The first 90 days, written from the outside

I have not seen your network, your contracts, or your ticket queue. So this is not a plan to fix things I have diagnosed from a job posting. It is a plan to find out what is actually true, quickly, and to earn the right to propose anything at all.

Listen, and inventory what exists

Goal: replace assumptions with facts, including mine

  • Sit down individually with every member of the technology team. What is broken that you have stopped reporting, and what would you fix if someone let you?
  • Visit all ten sites and the preschool center. Ride along on tickets rather than reading about them.
  • Meet every principal and ask one question: what does technology cost you in time each week?
  • Build the inventory that decisions require: network topology, licenses, contracts with renewal dates, warranty and refresh status, backup configuration and last successful restore, SIS integrations, E-Rate funding year status and who owns the filing.
  • Read three years of ticket history and find the pattern rather than the anecdotes.
  • Ask the cabinet what the last technology plan promised that has not been delivered, and why.

Stabilize, and put numbers on the promises

Goal: make the department measurable and predictable

  • Publish service levels people can hold me to: ticket categories, response targets, and what counts as an emergency, agreed with principals rather than announced to them.
  • Verify the backups by restoring from them. Document what worked, what did not, and what it would cost to close the gap.
  • Run the first tabletop exercise on the continuity plan with business, educational services, and site leadership in the room.
  • Document the standardization gaps that cost the team the most support hours, and fix the cheapest one immediately.
  • Review every contract renewing in the next twelve months before it renews itself.
  • Brief the cabinet in plain language, with no acronyms that need translating.

Commit to a roadmap, and deliver something visible

Goal: a plan tied to money, dates, and owners

  • Present a three-year technology roadmap aligned to the budget cycle and the E-Rate calendar, with the trade-offs stated openly rather than buried.
  • Set a device refresh schedule that is funded rather than aspirational.
  • Put student data privacy, acceptable use, and vendor review into written procedure, including how the district evaluates AI tools before staff adopt them anyway.
  • Define what the team is accountable for and what I am accountable for, in writing.
  • Deliver one improvement a teacher would notice without being told about it.

Where I would put my attention first

The unglamorous half of this job is the half that matters

Districts the size of National School District are now targeted deliberately. Attackers know that a small district holds the same student records as a large one and usually has a fraction of the staff protecting them.

Business continuity is not a binder on a shelf. It is a set of decisions made before the incident, tested on a schedule, and owned by a person with a name.

I came into education technology from a digital forensics lab, which means I learned this work backwards: I saw the aftermath before I ever ran the prevention. That order of experience leaves you with a specific habit. You stop asking whether a control exists and start asking when it was last tested, by whom, and what the evidence looks like. I would bring that habit here, and I would report the answers honestly even when they are uncomfortable, because a superintendent who learns about a gap during the incident has been failed by the person in this chair.

Questions I would bring to day one

What I would want to know before I proposed anything

I think you learn more about a candidate from the questions they ask than the answers they rehearse. These are mine.

What breaks most often, and who on staff currently absorbs it quietly instead of reporting it?
When was the last full restore test, and did it succeed?
Which vendor contracts renew in the next twelve months, and has anyone read them recently?
Where is the district in the current E-Rate funding year, and who owns the filing calendar?
Which systems do site staff work around rather than through, and what does the workaround cost them?
What did the last technology plan promise that was not delivered, and what got in the way?
What does the department get blamed for that is not actually theirs to control?
If I could fix exactly one thing in year one, what would each principal choose?
